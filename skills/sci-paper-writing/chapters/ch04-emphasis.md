# Chapter 4: Emphasis

## Core Idea
The beginning and end of a sentence are the most emphatic positions. Put familiar information at the beginning (topic position) and important new information at the end (stress position).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Topic and Stress**: Beginning = topic position (familiar info); End = stress position (important new info).
- **Managing Endings**: (1) Trim the end - delete trailing words. (2) Shift less important info to the left. (3) Use punctuation (colon, dash) to mark emphasis.
- **Sentence-End Weight**: Long complex elements at the end; short simple elements at the beginning.

## Mental Models
- Stress position: the end is where readers pause and absorb.
- Seesaw: light at the beginning, heavy at the end.
- Trim-shift: first try deleting trailing words; if that fails, move them left.

## Anti-patterns
- **Weak endings**: Ending with unimportant words (for the most part, in a few words).
- **Top-heavy sentences**: Long subjects followed by short predicates.
- **Buried key information**: Important point in the middle.

## Worked Example
Before: No one can explain how the arms race between a host and a pathogen is co-evolved in a few words.
After: No one can explain in a few words how the arms race between a host and a pathogen is co-evolved.

Analysis: in a few words moved from end to mid-sentence, exposing key point in stress position.

Before: Some transcription factors play crucial roles in response to biotic and abiotic stresses from bacteria, fungi, virus, nematodes, drought, salt, high temperature, and so on.
After: Some transcription factors play crucial roles in response to biotic and abiotic stresses.
Analysis: Everything after biotic and abiotic stresses is redundant - trimmed.

## Key Takeaways
1. The end of a sentence is the most emphatic position
2. Trim unnecessary trailing words to expose key points
3. Shift less important information leftward
4. Keep subjects short; put complexity in predicate
5. Use punctuation to mark emphasis deliberately
